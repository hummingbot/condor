# Condor — product & business strategy: monetization and target market

Companion to [`docs/architecture/agent-framework.md`](../architecture/agent-framework.md),
which covers what Condor *is* technically (the assistant/agent/skill/routine/
strategy framework, session mechanics). This doc addresses what the
architecture doc deliberately doesn't: how Condor makes money, who it's for,
and whether it stays open source. It's a research-and-recommend doc, not a
decided plan — treat the recommendation as a starting position to argue with.

## 1. Where Condor actually stands today

Before comparing external models, the baseline matters more than any of them:

- **`condor.hummingbot.org` is already live**, not a placeholder or future ICO
  page. It's a working docs/marketing site — "an open source harness for
  deploying and managing fleets of AI trading agents" — with install guides,
  a launch blog post ("Introducing the Hummingbot Harness for Trading
  Agents"), an active GitHub repo (`hummingbot/condor`), and even a podcast
  series. It ships today as free, self-hosted OSS via a `curl | bash`
  quickstart with Telegram bot setup. Any monetization plan has to reconcile
  with the fact that Condor is *already* publicly positioned as free and open.
- **Hummingbot Foundation** (the parent org) is a Cayman Islands nonprofit,
  spun out of the for-profit CoinAlpha in late 2021, mission "democratize
  access to high-frequency trading." It is **not** currently a product
  business:
  - **HBOT** (its ERC-20 token, 1B fixed supply, trades on Uniswap/Bitrue) is
    **governance-only** — 1 token = 1 vote on PRs/proposals via Snapshot. The
    Foundation explicitly states it does not fund itself by selling HBOT.
  - **Hummingbot Miner**, the original liquidity-mining incentive program tied
    to HBOT distribution, **shut down March 16, 2026** — that chapter is
    closed, not a live precedent.
  - **Actual revenue** comes from *exchange fee-share partnerships* (a cut of
    trading fees generated by Hummingbot users, at no cost to the user) and
    *bounty/administration fees* for managing exchange-funded connector
    development. Disclosed revenue: $423K (2022) → $309K (2023), during the
    downturn; described as recovering through 2024–2025.
  - No cloud/enterprise/paid-dashboard product exists anywhere in the
    ecosystem today. The Hummingbot Dashboard is free and community-built.
- **Botcamp Inc.** is a separate, existing for-profit entity in the same
  ecosystem — confirmed distinct from the nonprofit Foundation, run by the
  same two people (Michael Feng and Federico Cardoso, who is also the
  Foundation's CTO), explicitly split off "to minimize conflicts of interest"
  between for-profit and nonprofit activity. Today it runs a paid education
  business: cohort-based bootcamps (5 weeks, ~40 students/cohort, now on
  cohort 13–14, 200+ alumni since Oct 2022) plus a self-paced course
  library and monthly membership (content, live sessions, mentorship, "Bot
  Battles" competitions), billed via a $400 deposit + tuition model with
  invoice-based group/corporate billing. The Foundation's own "State of the
  Foundation 2024" post credits Botcamp with making the Foundation
  "sustainable in large part" and discloses an outstanding loan between the
  two entities. **Correction to an earlier pass of this research**: Botcamp
  is not education-only — it also runs **Botcamp Solutions**
  (botcamp.xyz/solutions), a client-services market-making/liquidity
  business: "custom liquidity solutions on any CEX, DEX, and chain," built
  on open-source Hummingbot infrastructure, marketed on "no black boxes."
  Named clients include **Ripple, MetaDAO, and USDM1 (M1X Global)** — and
  this repo is *already* dogfooding that USDM1 mandate today
  (`routines/usdm_report.py`, `usdm_arb_execute.py`, `usdm_lp_refresh.py`,
  `usdm_orca_center.py`). This is a significant fact for the roadmap (see
  §7): Botcamp Inc. already has the thing a new for-profit product needs: a for-profit legal
  entity, working billing infrastructure, and an existing paying
  Hummingbot-adjacent customer base to sell into.

**Implication**: Condor isn't choosing a monetization model in a vacuum — it's
choosing one that either (a) extends the Foundation's existing nonprofit,
fee-share-funded, fully-open posture, or (b) breaks from it, which is a real
organizational/legal decision (a separate for-profit entity, a different
governance structure, a relationship to HBOT to define), not just a product
choice. That tension runs through every option below.

## 2. Comparable landscape

Four distinct monetization philosophies, from the projects named in the
research:

| | Model | Open source? | Who pays | Fit for Condor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **OpenClaw** | Foundation/sponsor-funded, no product revenue | Yes (MIT), fully | Nobody — corporate sponsors (OpenAI, GitHub, NVIDIA, Vercel...) + GitHub Sponsors donations, "a passion project, not a business," funds explicitly recycled to contributors | Closest to Condor's *current* state, but a dead end if the goal is actually to monetize |
| **Nous Research / Hermes** | Open-core + paid hosted layer | Yes (MIT) for the agent framework | Free tier ($0, eval-only, no Tool Gateway); **Plus $20/mo → $22 credit**; **Super $100/mo → $110 credit**; **Ultra $200/mo → $220 credit** — a consistent ~10% bonus-credit structure across tiers, all paid tiers bundling the Tool Gateway (web search, image gen, TTS, browser automation). *Confidence: tier prices are high-confidence third-party consensus (3+ independent sources agree), not vendor-page-confirmed — Nous's own pricing page was rate-limited on every fetch attempt.* No disclosed markup ratio, no annual discount, no enterprise tier found. | **Closest structural match** — VC-backed for-profit, free framework, paid convenience layer |
| **Harvey.ai** | Fully closed, vertical enterprise SaaS | No — zero OSS anywhere | BigLaw/Big Four institutions only, opaque $50K–300K+/yr contracts, no self-serve tier, no public pricing | Proves the vertical-AI-for-institutions ceiling is huge ($11B valuation on ~$190M ARR) but requires abandoning OSS entirely and building an enterprise sales motion Condor doesn't have |
| **Venice.ai ($VVV)** | Open product, optional crypto-native payment rail | Product itself isn't OSS, but token model is the relevant pattern | Stake VVV → proportional daily "Diem" compute credits; non-holders just pay a plain $18/mo fiat subscription — **token ownership is optional, not required** | A *payment-rail* pattern that could sit on top of an open-core model without requiring a full ICO |
| **MetaDAO (futarchy ICO)** | Governance/fundraising framework, project-agnostic | N/A — a launch mechanism, not a product | Public futarchy-market-based capital raise; the "company" becomes governed by conditional-market votes on proposals | Highest optionality/marketing, but highest constraint and risk (see §4) |

Two details from the research worth carrying into the decision:

- **Venice's token is genuinely optional** — the model works *because*
  non-holders can just pay cash. That's very different from "you must hold a
  token to use the product," which is the model MetaDAO-style ICOs often
  imply. This distinction matters a lot for Condor given its existing
  non-crypto-native individual traders.
- **MetaDAO's legal-ownership story is young and unsettled** — "ownership
  coins" exist to tie token-holding to real legal/revenue claims, but this is
  new, largely untested by case law, and MetaDAO itself recommends pairing it
  with a conventional foundation + DAO LLC, not replacing one. Track record:
  ~$96M raised across projects by Dec 2025 — real, but still early.

## 3. The three key decisions

### 3a. Open source or closed source?

Condor is already public, MIT-quality-open, and carries the Hummingbot
Foundation's "democratize access" mission. Going fully closed (the Harvey
path) would be a brand and mission reversal — it would contradict the parent
org's charter and very likely fracture the existing Hummingbot community that
Condor draws its initial audience from. It would also forfeit the thing that
makes a framework-not-just-an-agent valuable: third-party skills/routines/
strategies contributed the way Hummingbot connectors are today (see the
architecture doc's skill/routine ontology — that plugin surface *is* the
community-contribution surface, the same lever Hummingbot itself already
pulled successfully).

This still holds under an even harder assumption — that any Condor codebase
gets copied by competitors regardless of license. §10 works through that
"code is copyable" scenario directly: the real moat was never going to be
the code either way; it's brand/community/distribution Condor already has,
plus (if pursued) the capital and liquidity network effects of §9's
tokenized-agent model, which a fork can't replicate on day one. That
analysis argues for taking §9 seriously as a direction, not for reversing
the open-source call made here.

**Recommendation: stay open source at the framework/reference-agent layer —
that's Condor's actual identity — and monetize a layer on top (open-core),
following Nous's pattern rather than Harvey's or OpenClaw's.** Fully closed
throws away the one asset (community, trust, existing distribution) Condor
already has; fully donation-funded (OpenClaw) is a legitimate choice but is
explicitly *not* a business, which conflicts with "how do we monetize."

### 3b. Individual, institutional, or both?

- **Individual/prosumer** is the audience Condor already has (Hummingbot's
  existing retail/prosumer algo-trader base), fits the current Telegram-bot-
  first UX, and matches a self-serve, metered/subscription monetization model
  (Venice-style credits or a plain hosted-tier subscription) with low sales
  overhead.
- **Institutional** has the higher ceiling (Harvey's $50K–300K+/yr contracts,
  $11B valuation) but requires things Condor and the Foundation don't have
  today: a dedicated enterprise sales motion, compliance/audit posture (SOC2-
  equivalent), and — given the session architecture documented in the
  architecture doc (per-user subprocess, auto-approved delegate execution,
  no built-in multi-tenant hardening) — real security engineering before
  it's defensible to a prop desk or fund. Institutions evaluating an
  LLM-driven autonomous trading agent will also scrutinize explainability/
  audit trails harder than an individual self-hosting the same code for
  their own capital.

**Recommendation: individual/prosumer first, institutional later, not both at
once.** Both Harvey and Nous succeeded by picking one audience cleanly rather
than straddling; Condor's current architecture, UX, and org capacity fit
individual users today, and a credible institutional motion is a distinct,
later, heavier-weight build (support contracts, security hardening, sales
hires) rather than a parallel track from day one.

### 3c. Should Condor host instances?

Yes — this is the single highest-leverage monetization decision available,
and it's the mechanism that makes open-core work at all. The architecture
doc's session-mechanics section (§6) already shows *why* self-hosting is
nontrivial for a typical user: each active chat spawns a real OS-process tree
(`claude-agent-acp → claude → MCP servers`), needs exchange API keys
configured, and requires babysitting a long-lived subprocess. That
operational complexity is exactly what a paid hosted tier sells — "the code
is free forever, but running it reliably, with your keys, at scale, without
managing subprocesses yourself, costs money" — precisely Nous's Portal
argument, and structurally identical to why Venice's Pro tier exists
alongside its free/self-serve API.

Hosting does introduce a real new liability surface the Foundation doesn't
currently carry (custody of user exchange API keys, uptime/security
responsibility for other people's trading capital) — this needs explicit
security/legal review before launch, not an afterthought.

## 4. Who runs this: resolving the entity question via Botcamp Inc.

Section 6 originally flagged an open question: does Condor monetization
happen inside the nonprofit Foundation, or does it need a separate for-profit
entity? **Resolved: route it through Botcamp Inc.**, not a new entity.

Reasoning: Botcamp Inc. already exists for exactly this purpose. It was
deliberately split out from the Foundation as a for-profit vehicle to avoid
exactly the conflict-of-interest problem a hosted Condor product would
otherwise create if run *inside* a nonprofit whose charter is "democratize
free access." It shares leadership with the Foundation (Feng, Cardoso), so
there's no new-relationship risk, no new legal-entity formation needed, and
no governance ambiguity to resolve before shipping — it's already the
established for-profit counterpart to the Foundation. It also already has
billing infrastructure (course/cohort payments, invoice-based group billing)
and an existing paying customer base (bootcamp alumni and members) who are
close to the ideal first customer for a hosted Condor product: people who
already committed money to learning Hummingbot strategy development and would
plausibly pay to run the result without operating their own server.

This also cleanly answers the token/ICO question raised in the comparable
landscape (§2): *if* Condor ever pursues a credits token or MetaDAO-style
launch (Stage 3, §5), Botcamp Inc. — not the Foundation — is the entity that
would do it, since the Foundation doesn't sell tokens and has its own
governance-only HBOT token already. That's a decision for Stage 3, not now,
but it no longer blocks Stage 1.

Recommended arrangement: **Botcamp Inc. builds, bills, and operates the
hosted Condor product**; the open-source framework and reference agent
remain stewarded by the Foundation, unchanged. A portion of gross margin
should flow back to the Foundation (formalizing the support relationship the
"State of the Foundation 2024" post already discloses as a loan) so the
nonprofit's OSS stewardship isn't left funding a for-profit product's success
for nothing in return — the exact split is a governance decision for Feng/
Cardoso and the Foundation board, not something this doc can resolve, but it
should be made explicit rather than left as an informal loan.

## 5. Hosted managed Condor instances — product spec

This is the flagship Stage 1 monetization lever from §2/§4 (now §6/§8 below),
fleshed out concretely: what it is, why it's the natural product, who buys
it, and at what price.

### What it is

A fully managed deployment of the open-source Condor stack, run by Botcamp
Inc. on the customer's behalf, so they never touch the operational layer
documented in the architecture doc's session-mechanics section (§6): no
`curl | bash` setup, no babysitting a long-lived `claude-agent-acp` subprocess
tree, no renting/managing a VPS to keep a `TickEngine` strategy ticking 24/7,
no separately metering and paying their own LLM API bill. Concretely, a
hosted instance includes:

- **An always-on host** for the user's Condor assistant session and any
  autonomous agent strategies (`agents/{slug}/strategies/`) — the single
  biggest reason self-hosting is a real barrier: a `TickEngine` loop needs a
  server that's on at 2am, which most individual traders don't want to run
  themselves.
- **Bundled model/compute cost** — the subscription (not a separate API key)
  covers the underlying LLM calls, the same way Nous Portal bundles model
  costs into a credits allowance rather than requiring the user's own
  Anthropic/OpenAI key.
- **Managed exchange connectivity** via the user's own exchange API keys
  (custodied, not Botcamp's) plus Hummingbot execution — and, as an upsell,
  **premium data/RPC add-ons**: faster/private RPC endpoints and premium data
  feeds beyond the free public defaults Gateway uses. This is a genuinely
  validated cost center, not a hypothetical one — private RPC providers
  (e.g. QuickNode-style dedicated endpoints) are a real expense individual
  power users already pay for out of pocket today; bundling/reselling that
  as a paid tier feature turns an existing willingness-to-pay into revenue
  instead of leaving it as a DIY line item.
- **Reliability the self-hosted health-monitor can only do best-effort** —
  actual uptime SLA, and a managed shutdown-policy guarantee (the
  deterministic-close-out-first sequence from the architecture doc's §5) that
  fires even if the user's own machine or connection is down.
- **Support**, scaled by tier (community → email → dedicated channel →
  white-glove).

### Target customers (staged, individual-first per §3b)

1. **Existing Hummingbot retail/prosumer users** who want an always-on agent
   without renting/managing their own server — the largest, most
   product-market-fit-aligned segment, and the one the current architecture
   and Telegram-first UX already serve.
2. **Botcamp alumni and members** — a warm, already-paying funnel. Natural
   packaging: bundle a discounted or included hosted Condor seat into
   cohort tuition/membership, converting course graduates directly into a
   recurring hosted-product customer rather than leaving them to
   self-host what they just learned to build.
3. **Small trading pods/teams (2–5 people)** — a "Team" tier bridges toward
   institutional demand without requiring an enterprise sales motion: shared
   skill/strategy library, multiple seats, team audit logs.
4. **(Stage 2+, light-touch only)** small funds/prop desks wanting a custom
   agent built on the open framework — sold as a Botcamp Inc. consulting
   engagement layered on top of a Team/Enterprise hosted instance, not a
   separate enterprise product line from day one.

### Pricing tiers

Anchored against Nous Portal's confirmed structure (§2) — comparable
subscription-to-bundled-credit ratios — but priced higher per tier than a
pure chat/tool product, since a trading agent's marginal cost also includes
continuous tick-loop compute and exchange/data connectivity, not just
on-demand chat inference:

| Tier | Price | What's included |
|---|---|---|
| **Free / Self-hosted** | $0 | The status quo: full OSS framework, self-hosted, bring-your-own LLM key and server. No change — this stays free forever, it's the open-core promise. |
| **Starter** | ~$29–49/mo | One hosted Condor chat session + one autonomous strategy (one `TickEngine` loop, one exchange/pair), standard tick frequency, a bundled monthly compute-credit allowance (~10% bonus credit, Nous-style) with metered overage beyond it, community + email support, best-effort uptime. |
| **Pro** | ~$99–199/mo | Multiple concurrent strategies (e.g. up to 5), higher tick frequency, larger compute-credit allowance, premium data/RPC add-on available, priority support, custom-skill-authoring assistance. |
| **Team** | ~$499–999/mo | Multiple seats, shared skill/strategy library, team audit logs/session history, higher uptime SLA (e.g. 99.9%), dedicated support channel (Telegram/Slack). |
| **Enterprise / custom** | "Contact sales" | White-glove custom strategy development (a Botcamp Inc. consulting engagement), dedicated infrastructure isolation, compliance/audit-trail exports, custom SLA. Stage 2+ only — see §7. |

Usage-based add-ons available at any tier: extra compute/agent-turn credits
beyond the bundled allowance, premium data feeds/RPC endpoints, extra
concurrent strategies beyond the tier cap — metered the way Nous meters
Tool Gateway usage against the base subscription, not folded silently into
the flat price.

## 6. Monetization options, concretely

Ranked roughly by how directly each fits Condor's current state vs. how much
new organizational lift it requires:

1. **Hosted/managed Condor instances** (tiered by compute/model quota,
   concurrent-agent limits, uptime SLA). Open-core, Nous-style. Lowest lift,
   highest fit — this is the primary recommendation.
2. **Extend the Foundation's existing exchange fee-share model to
   Condor-executed volume.** The Foundation already has a working, identity-
   consistent revenue mechanism (a cut of trading fees, at no cost to the
   user) — routing Condor-managed-agent trade flow through the same
   fee-share partnerships is close to zero new organizational surface area
   and requires no new pricing page, sales motion, or user-facing charge at
   all.
3. **Skills/strategies/routines marketplace** (revenue share on third-party
   plugins). Directly enabled by the plugin architecture already documented
   in the architecture doc — once there's a real base of third-party skill/
   strategy authors, take a cut the way an app store does. Requires the
   ecosystem to mature first; not a launch-day option.
4. **Light institutional motion: paid custom-agent-building / support
   contracts** for funds/prop desks that want a bespoke strategy built on the
   open framework — a much lighter version of Harvey's model that doesn't
   require closing the source, just adds a services layer on top.
5. **Credits/utility token, Venice-style** (stake for proportional compute
   credits; non-holders pay fiat). Optional-by-design, so it doesn't force
   crypto-nativeness on the existing audience — but it's a real product +
   legal + tokenomics build, appropriate only once there's a hosted product
   and real usage volume to back the credit economy.
6. **Full ICO / DAO ownership via MetaDAO.** Highest marketing/community
   upside, highest constraint: futarchy binds future business-model pivots to
   market approval, the legal-ownership wrapper is young and untested, U.S.
   securities risk is real even under the friendlier 2026 framework, and —
   specific to Condor — it raises an unresolved structural question: does
   this happen *inside* the nonprofit Foundation (which doesn't currently
   sell tokens and has its own HBOT governance token already), or does
   Condor spin out as a separate for-profit/DAO entity? That question has to
   be answered before this path is even actionable, independent of whether
   it's a good idea.

## 7. Recommended staged path

Revised after §9's research: rather than launching a new external product
and hoping for adoption, **validate first against a real, already-paying
business Botcamp Inc. runs today** — Botcamp Solutions — before committing to
either the ambitious tokenized-platform path or a traditional external SaaS
scale-out. This also directly answers §9d's cautionary lesson: CoinAlpha
Falcon failed in part because it went to market on unproven tech with no
real customer validating it first; this sequencing does the opposite.

**Stage 1 (now, ~0–6mo): validate Shape A internally via dogfooding, not as
an external launch.** Build the Swig-secured hosted-agent architecture (§9b
Shape A: Condor/Botcamp hosts the compute, the client/owner holds the Swig
owner key, the hosted agent process holds only a bounded delegate role) and
use it to run Botcamp Solutions' **existing** client mandates — Ripple,
MetaDAO, and USDM1/M1X Global are named clients today, and this repo is
already dogfooding the USDM1 relationship (`routines/usdm_*.py`). This
stage has two purposes at once: (a) prove the Condor agent/strategy
framework is actually the right technical solution for work Botcamp
Solutions already sells, using real capital and real usage, not a
hypothetical new customer; (b) upgrade an existing revenue line's trust
story — "your capital sits in a Swig wallet you control, not our ops team's
hot wallet" is a real, sellable improvement to a service Botcamp already
bills for, at zero incremental customer-acquisition cost, and with far lower
regulatory exposure than a tokenized-fund launch (it's a services fee
relationship, not a security). No public launch, no token, no new pricing
page — this is internal validation, deliberately quiet.

**Stage 2 (after Stage 1 validates, informed by real evidence): make the
fork decision.** Only once Shape A has actually run real client capital
reliably does it make sense to choose between:

- **(a) MetaDAO ICO for the tokenized-agent platform (Shape B, §9)** — made
  meaningfully lower-friction than a cold approach, since MetaDAO is already
  a Botcamp Solutions client relationship, not an unfamiliar counterparty.
  Still gated on the full legal review §9d demands before any public
  offering, and still subject to §2/§6's futarchy/pivot-constraint caveats.
- **(b) Scale Shape A into a traditional external managed-hosted-instances
  SaaS business** — open the same Swig-secured hosted-agent product to
  retail/prosumer users and additional market-making clients beyond
  Botcamp Solutions' current roster (the pricing/tiers/target-customer
  design in §5), with the Foundation's exchange fee-share model (option 2)
  extended to Condor volume as a low-lift secondary stream.
- **(c) A technical/compliance partnership with Enzyme Finance, focused on
  Solana (§9e)** — an alternative route to Shape B's tokenized-fund model
  that reaches for the same capital/liquidity moat (§10) without running a
  public token event at all: Condor supplies the Solana-native AI-agent
  execution layer, a proven partner supplies 9 years of vault/fee/policy
  engine credibility and — critically — real regulated-fund compliance
  precedent (Avantgarde, CV5 Capital) that directly answers §9d's Falcon
  lesson. Carries real counterparty/business-development risk (no evidence
  Enzyme has Solana plans today), and is not mutually exclusive with (a) or
  (b) — it's a different way to build the same underlying vault/custody
  layer §9's Shape B needs, evaluated in §9e alongside building in-house or
  integrating GLAM.

These aren't mutually exclusive forever — (b) can run first, and (a) or (c)
can follow once there's a real hosted-usage base or a viable partner to
build the tokenized layer against — but do not commit engineering or legal
spend to any of them before Stage 1 has produced real evidence.

**Stage 3 (12–24mo+): expand.** Once third-party skill/strategy authorship is
real, add a marketplace with revenue share (option 3). Begin a *light*
institutional motion — paid custom-agent-building/support contracts (option
4) — without closing the source. If (a) was chosen in Stage 2 and is
working, a Venice-style optional credits token (option 5) can layer on top
of the hosted product at this point too.

## 9. A radically different lever: tokenized trading-agent creation on Solana

> **Status: deferred, not in the active roadmap** — see the note at the top
> of §7. Swig integration and tokenization are excluded from
> [`roadmap-v2.md`](./roadmap-v2.md)'s current phases; this section is
> preserved as research to revisit later, not a live plan.

This is a distinct track from §§4–7, not a refinement of the hosted-instances
plan — evaluated separately because its risk profile (capital custody,
securities law) is categorically different from a SaaS subscription.

### 9a. The building blocks that actually exist today

This isn't hypothetical — there's already a mature, mainnet-verified
implementation to build on. The Gateway fork's `feat/swig-solana-clean`
branch implements **Swig**, a Solana smart-contract wallet, specifically to
solve: *"limit the blast radius if the trading server is breached."*
Mechanically (`src/wallet/swig/README.md` in the gateway repo):

- An **owner/root key** (kept offline — hardware wallet or treasury) creates
  the wallet and grants a **delegate role** to the key that actually signs
  trades — the one that has to live on a server to trade autonomously.
- The delegate is bounded by an on-chain, **default-deny** policy: a program
  allowlist (which venues it may invoke — Orca, Meteora, Raydium confirmed
  working; **Jupiter is the one exception**, since its aggregator routes
  through too many programs to allowlist individually, so a Jupiter-enabled
  delegate needs an unrestricted-program/token-cap-only role instead), a
  per-mint spend cap (exact amounts, not just token allowlisting), and a
  small one-time SOL cap for fees/rent.
- Caps bound the **amount**, not the destination — pinning a destination
  breaks swaps (which pay a pool vault, not the owner). So the accurate
  framing of the original idea is "restrict which venues/programs and how
  much," not "restrict which wallet address it can send to."
- **Fully revocable**: the owner can rotate/remove the delegate at any time,
  bounding worst-case loss to `enabled mints × caps + the SOL cap` — never
  "all funds." Verified on mainnet per the module's own README.

This is a genuine, already-built trust primitive for exactly the problem that
plagues the entire "AI agent trading" category: investors have no assurance
today that an agent (or its host) can't simply drain their funds.

**Metaplex is not the tool for the fund-share mechanic**, though — research
confirms its current products (Token Metadata, Core, Genesis, Hydra, the
now-archived Token Vault) provide token *metadata/branding* and, at most, a
generic fixed-supply fundraising bonding curve (Genesis) — none of them track
NAV or handle proportional redemption against a specific wallet's assets.

**The team doesn't need a third-party protocol for that mechanic — it already
built and ran one.** `CoinAlpha/fund-protocol` (2017, archived, 102 stars) is
exactly this: `Fund.sol` + `NavCalculator.sol` + `InvestorActions.sol` +
`DataFeed.sol` implement segregated custody, NAV-per-share calculation via an
oracle, subscription/redemption queueing, and — directly reusable as a fee
model — on-chain `adminFeeBps`, `mgmtFeeBps`, and `performFeeBps`: a
proper management-fee-plus-performance-fee structure, not a bonding-curve
tax. `CoinAlpha/basket-protocol` (2018, archived) is the complementary
piece: an Arranger/Supplier/Buyer role split with a Factory (creates new
basket contracts), Registry (tracks baskets minted/burned and usage per
arranger), and Escrow (buy/sell orders, order filling) — a template for
"many tokenized instruments, each tracked in a registry, each tradeable,"
which maps directly onto "many tokenized Condor agents." Both are Solidity/
Ethereum and archived, so this is design and lesson **prior art to port to
Solana (Anchor/Rust) combined with Swig for custody**, not a reusable
library — but it's a materially stronger starting point than borrowing
GLAM Protocol's design from scratch, since the team already owns the IP,
the design decisions, and — critically, see §9d — the lived experience of
what happened when this exact model was taken to market.

### 9b. Two product shapes with very different risk profiles

- **Shape A — Swig-secured custody within the hosted product, no tokenized
  shares.** This is **not** self-hosting — Condor/Botcamp still hosts the
  compute (the always-on strategy loop, the reliability, everything in §5).
  What changes is only *who controls the capital*: the client/user holds the
  Swig **owner** key, the Condor-hosted agent process holds only a bounded
  **delegate** role (allowlisted venues, per-mint caps, revocable anytime).
  So it's "hosted, but the client never has to fully trust Botcamp's ops
  team with unbounded control of their funds" — a custody upgrade layered
  onto the existing hosted product, not a separate self-hosted offering and
  not a new business model. No fundraising, no tokenized shares, essentially
  no incremental securities exposure — it can ship as a differentiated tier
  (marketed on the real, mainnet-verified guarantee) well before Shape B.
- **Shape B — tokenized agent fund / creation-fee launchpad**, the ambitious
  version actually being asked about here: outside investors deposit capital
  into a Swig-secured wallet in exchange for a token representing a claim on
  that agent's P&L, tradeable on the same DEXs (Phoenix, Orca, Meteora,
  Pump, Jupiter) the agent itself trades on. Condor/Botcamp charges a
  creation fee per agent and/or an ongoing cut of trading volume or AUM.

### 9c. The closest live comparable: Virtuals Protocol

Virtuals Protocol (Base) is the nearest real-world precedent for "platform
where users tokenize an AI agent and the platform takes a cut." Its
mechanics: a 100 $VIRTUAL creation fee spins up an agent + wallet + token,
paired on a bonding curve; a 1% trading tax splits ~70/30 to the
creator/platform; the token "graduates" to a real DEX pool once the curve
fills. Cumulative protocol revenue reached roughly $60M at peak (~2025),
with one breakout agent (aixbt) briefly approaching a $1B token valuation.

**The critical design detail to note, not copy blindly**: Virtuals tokens
carry **no formal claim on agent revenue** — proceeds are recycled into
buyback/burn, not distributed as profit share, specifically to keep the
token out of "investment contract" territory under Howey's "efforts of
others" prong. Even with that deliberate hedge, the category still draws
"99% of these are a scam" criticism from credible on-chain analysts, aixbt
is down ~97% from its peak, and it suffered a real security exploit.

**This directly bears on Shape B**: the model as originally described here —
investors get an actual claim on a specific agent's P&L — is a *more*
securities-exposed design than Virtuals' already-scrutinized one, precisely
because it includes the profit-claim element Virtuals went out of its way to
avoid. Copying Virtuals' fee mechanics without copying its no-profit-claim
structure would mean inheriting Virtuals' regulatory exposure minus the one
hedge Virtuals built in against it.

### 9d. This has been tried before, by this team: CoinAlpha Falcon, LP

Before treating Shape B as new territory: CoinAlpha (Hummingbot's own
predecessor entity, founded by Michael Feng in 2017) already built and ran
almost exactly this model once, and the outcome is directly instructive —
not just prior art in the code, but a lived result.

CoinAlpha Falcon, LP was one of the first tokenized hedge funds, built on
`fund-protocol`: full SEC registration, a Private Placement Memorandum, and
investor vetting, with smart contracts substituting for a traditional
administrator/auditor (NAV calculation, subscription/redemption, fee
accounting all on-chain). It achieved daily liquidity and low minimums — the
efficiency case worked technically. It still didn't succeed, for two
distinct reasons worth separating:

- **Demand-side rejection, not a technical failure.** Target investors —
  traditional/institutional LPs — didn't want it. One traditional hedge fund
  manager's own words, as quoted in Hummingbot's origin-story retrospective:
  *"If I told my LPs we were using smart contracts, they would probably
  redeem because they would think we're not spending time on strategy."*
  Institutional LPs wanted "the rubber-stamp of a third-party administrator,"
  which the entire pitch was designed to eliminate.
- **Real regulatory friction**, separate from the demand problem: an SEC
  letter citing "general solicitation" (publicly discussing a private
  securities offering — a specific, well-known Reg D trap), settled with
  regulators. The founders' own retrospective lesson: *"the 'move fast and
  break things' approach doesn't work"* in finance.

A third finding bears directly on §10's moat question, independently of this
specific product decision: by 2018 the team concluded *"smart contracts
became a relatively fungible skill"* while reliable multi-exchange trading
infrastructure was *"much harder"* — which is exactly why they pivoted to
Hummingbot instead. In their own assessment, the tokenization/smart-contract
layer itself was not where the durable value was.

**Why Shape B could plausibly go differently this time, and what must not be
repeated:**

- **Different audience.** Falcon targeted traditional institutional LPs who
  explicitly wanted a human administrator as a trust signal. A Solana-native
  Shape B targeting crypto-native retail (§3b's own recommended audience)
  is pitching the opposite audience — one that, per the Virtuals precedent
  (§9c), already prefers on-chain, no-administrator, no-black-box structures.
  The demand-side objection that killed Falcon may simply not apply to this
  audience — but this is a hypothesis to validate, not an assumption to build
  on.
- **The regulatory mistake must not be repeated verbatim.** "General
  solicitation" is a specific, avoidable trap (publicly marketing what is
  legally a private securities offering) — any Shape B legal review must
  explicitly design around this, not just generically "consult a lawyer."
- **The moat won't come from the tokenization mechanic itself** — the
  team already learned that lesson once. Per §10, if Shape B is pursued, the
  durable value has to come from captured capital/liquidity network effects
  and the real Swig-based trust guarantee, not from the smart-contract
  wrapper being clever.

### 9e. Build, integrate with GLAM, or partner with Enzyme — the vault-engine options

Three genuinely different ways to get the NAV/fee/subscription/redemption
layer underneath Shape B, now that all three have been researched directly:

| | Build in-house (`fund-protocol`/`basket-protocol` ported to Solana + Swig) | Integrate with GLAM Protocol | Partner with Enzyme Finance (Solana-focused) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Track record | None yet on Solana — the Ethereum original is 2017-era and archived | Live on Solana, real integrations (Drift, Kamino, Jupiter, Orca, Meteora...), but **no confirmed audit, TVL, or vault count found** | ~9 years continuous operation (Melon 2016 → Enzyme 2020), 1,400+ vaults, real (if modest) TVL, two disclosed bugs both caught pre-exploit via bug bounty |
| Technical fit | Full control, but full engineering + audit burden from zero | **Closest ready-made match**: instruction/program-scoped delegate ACLs *and* a documented, working AI-agent reference build (an AutoGen two-agent system rebalancing a vault via Jupiter, with slippage/tx-size limits enforced) | No Solana product exists — Enzyme is EVM-only; a "Solana-focused partnership" is realistically a fresh build informed by their architecture/compliance patterns, not shared code |
| Compliance precedent | None — has to be built from scratch, same gap that hurt Falcon | Unconfirmed/thin | **Real**: Avantgarde wraps Enzyme vaults into regulated fund products; CV5 Capital runs CIMA-regulated tokenized funds on "Enzyme Onyx" — an actual template for the exact structuring problem that killed Falcon |
| Ownership/moat | Team owns the IP outright | Dependent on a third-party protocol of unconfirmed maturity | Dependent on a partner who has no current Solana presence or demonstrated interest — real business-development risk |
| Fee model | Whatever's designed | Manager-configurable mgmt/perf/sub/redemption fees confirmed; protocol-level cut unconfirmed | **Confirmed two-layer model** (see below) — manager sets fees on depositors, Enzyme takes a separate, smaller protocol-level AUM fee — proven durable across 9 years |

**Two findings worth adopting regardless of which column is chosen:**

- **Enzyme's two-layer fee model is worth copying on its own, independent of
  any partnership.** Strategy creators set their own management/performance/
  subscription/redemption fees on their own depositors; Condor/Botcamp
  separately takes a smaller, protocol-level fee (e.g. an AUM-bps cut) —
  kept as two distinct fee lines rather than Condor taking a percentage of
  the creator's own fee. This cleanly separates "what the strategy creator
  earns for their skill" from "what Condor earns for the engine," and has
  survived Enzyme's entire 9-year run.
- **A real technical partnership with Enzyme is a plausible Stage 2 fork
  option in its own right** (see §7) — not just an alternative to building
  in-house, but a genuine **alternative to the MetaDAO ICO path**. It
  achieves the same underlying goal — a durable capital/liquidity moat per
  §10 — without a public token event at all: Condor would supply the
  Solana-native AI-agent execution layer (Swig integration, the
  agent/strategy/skill framework, Botcamp Solutions' real client
  relationships) to plug into Enzyme's proven vault/fee/policy engine and
  its regulated-fund compliance precedent, the same role Avantgarde's
  `enzyme-bot` or the Trading Strategy Python partnership already play on
  Ethereum today. This sidesteps §2 and §9d's biggest risks (futarchy
  pivot-lock-in, an untested legal-ownership wrapper, repeating Falcon's
  "general solicitation" mistake) by importing an already-proven compliant
  structure instead of inventing one — at the cost of real counterparty
  and business-development risk, since no evidence exists that Enzyme has
  Solana plans or would prioritize this partnership.

### 9f. Recommendation

1. **Ship Shape A now**, but internally first — see §7's revised sequencing:
   validate it by dogfooding Botcamp Solutions' existing client mandates
   (Ripple, MetaDAO, USDM1/M1X) before any external/public launch. This
   directly avoids Falcon's core mistake of going to market before the
   model was proven against a real, committed customer.
2. **Treat Shape B as a genuine later-stage initiative, gated on securities-
   law review before any engineering investment** — not because the
   mechanism is bad, but because Falcon already proved what happens when a
   tokenized-fund structure meets regulators without that review done first.
   The specific question counsel needs to answer: accredited-investor-only
   private placement? Non-US-only? Non-transferable membership interests
   instead of a tradeable token? And explicitly: how does the marketing plan
   avoid Falcon's "general solicitation" letter this time? An Enzyme-style
   partnership (§9e) is one way to answer this question by import rather
   than invention.
3. **Adopt the two-layer fee model (§9e) now**, in the pricing design for §5
   as well as any future Shape B structure — it's a no-regret decision that
   doesn't depend on the build/GLAM/Enzyme choice.
4. **Don't pick build-vs-GLAM-vs-Enzyme until Stage 2** (§7) — evaluate GLAM's
   actual audit/scale status directly (thin public evidence today) and
   whether Enzyme has any genuine appetite for Solana before committing
   engineering time to either; `fund-protocol`/`basket-protocol` remain the
   fallback design blueprint the team owns outright if neither external
   option proves out.

## 10. Open-source moat under a "code gets copied" assumption

Direct answer to the follow-up question: assume any Condor repo state can be
forked verbatim by a competitor tomorrow. What's actually defensible, and
does that change the open/closed-source or fundraising recommendation?

**What survives code being copied:**

- **Brand, community, and distribution** — Condor inherits Hummingbot's
  multi-year community and existing user base; a fork starts at zero trust
  and zero users, even with identical code.
- **Hosted-ops convenience** (§5) — a real but comparatively soft moat. A
  well-funded competitor can rebuild the hosting/reliability layer over time;
  this is the Nous/GitLab/Databricks-style open-core moat, and it's genuinely
  contestable, not permanent.
- **Skill/strategy marketplace network effects** (§6, option 3) — a
  two-sided marketplace (third-party strategy authors + users) is hard to
  fork because forking the repo doesn't fork the supply side of contributors
  already publishing there.
- **Captured capital and creator/liquidity network effects (Shape B, §9)** —
  this is the strongest moat available, and it's categorically different
  from the others: it isn't about the code at all. If Condor becomes the
  venue where actual capital is pooled, actual agent tokens trade, and actual
  creator-investor trust relationships exist, a fork of the repo inherits
  none of that — it starts with zero deposited capital, zero agent tokens,
  zero liquidity, zero creator flow. This is the same reason pump.fun's code
  has been cloned repeatedly without denting its market share, and why
  Virtuals' bonding-curve mechanism being public knowledge hasn't produced a
  competitor with equivalent liquidity: **the moat was never the code, it
  was the accumulated capital/liquidity/trust sitting on top of it.**

This isn't just theory — §9d's CoinAlpha Falcon history independently
confirms it from the inside. The team's own conclusion after running a real
tokenized-fund business was that *"smart contracts became a relatively
fungible skill"* while reliable trading infrastructure was the actually hard,
defensible thing — which is precisely the same "code/mechanism isn't the
moat" lesson, learned firsthand a decade before this document, not inferred
from someone else's platform.

**So: yes, the moat argument meaningfully strengthens the case for pursuing
Shape B seriously** — it's the one lever here that converts freely-copyable
code into something a fork cannot replicate on day one. Pure hosted-SaaS on
open code (§5 alone) has a real but softer, more contestable moat.

**On MetaDAO/ICO specifically as a moat-building tool, not just a fundraise**:
partially yes, but it's a weaker and more temporary version of the same
effect. A token-driven raise can build an aligned, evangelist holder base and
a liquid market fast — also hard for a fork to replicate on day one — but
that's an *attention/marketing* head start, not a structural liquidity moat.
The risk: running a MetaDAO-style raise **before** Shape B's actual
liquidity-generating mechanic exists monetizes hype around a promise, not a
moat — the moat only crystallizes once real capital, liquidity, and
creator trust actually accumulate on the platform, not from the raise event
itself. And §2's caveats still apply in full: futarchy binds future pivots to
market approval, and the legal-ownership wrapper remains young and untested.

**Recommended sequencing**: build Shape A first (real, low-risk, ships now)
→ get Shape B's legal structure right and prove it at small/controlled scale
→ *then* consider a MetaDAO-style raise or community campaign layered on top
of a working Shape B, as an accelerant for a liquidity mechanic that already
works, not as a substitute for building one. The moat argument is a reason to
take Shape B seriously as a direction — it is not, by itself, a reason to
rush a token launch ahead of the mechanic that would actually make it
defensible.

## 11. Implications for the agent framework

Direct answer to the follow-up question: if any of §9's direction is taken
seriously, does the assistant/agent/skill/routine/strategy ontology in
[`docs/architecture/agent-framework.md`](../architecture/agent-framework.md)
need to change? Three concrete conclusions, plus one explicit non-change.

### 11a. Generalize skills to a global-plus-local model, like routines — do this regardless of the tokenization decision

The architecture doc already flagged this as an "improvement area, not a
bug" (its §2): skills are fully siloed per owner today, with no shared tier,
unlike routines' global-plus-per-agent-local split. The business reasoning
in this doc makes that gap load-bearing rather than a nice-to-have cleanup.

Enzyme's Adapters/Policies and GLAM's ACL/integration model are exactly a
**shared, composable library** that any vault manager draws on instead of
rebuilding DeFi-protocol integrations from scratch — it's a large part of
why either ecosystem could scale past a handful of vaults. If Condor moves
toward more strategy creators building on the same engine — whether that's
just more Botcamp Solutions mandates (Stage 1) or eventually external
strategy authors under a white-label/tokenized model (Stage 2+) — each one
needing to reinvent basic playbooks (regime detection, capital allocation,
inventory management, report-building) in a private silo is the same
mistake Enzyme and GLAM deliberately avoided by sharing that layer.

The fix mirrors routines' existing asymmetric model, not a full flattening
(the original concern about a skill assuming a specific `tools` allowlist is
still valid): add a **global skills tier** (generic, tool-agnostic building
blocks any agent/strategy can pull from) alongside the existing **agent-local
tier** (specializations tied to a specific agent's own tool allowlist, e.g.
`pmm_mister_deploy`'s dependence on `manage_controllers`/`manage_bots`).
This is a no-regret change: it serves the pure-hosted-SaaS path (§5/§7 Stage
1–2) exactly as much as any tokenized-platform path, since it's about
serving more strategies off one engine either way.

### 11b. Tokenize at the strategy layer, not the assistant or agent layer

Directly answering "tokenize the agent layer, or people build their own
assistants and tokenize those": neither is the right unit — **the strategy
is** (`agents/{slug}/strategies/{sslug}/`, per the architecture doc's own
`(agent_slug, slug)` composite key).

- **The assistant layer is too shallow a unit.** Condor-the-coordinator
  doesn't hold or execute capital itself — it routes to skills and agents.
  Tokenizing "your own assistant" would tokenize a UX/persona layer, not an
  investable claim on anything. This is the wrong place to attach a Swig
  wallet or a Mint.
- **The agent layer is too coarse a unit.** An agent is a shared brain that
  can run *multiple* strategies (already established in the architecture
  doc — `StrategyStore.list()` returns a list, not a singleton). Tokenizing
  at the agent level would commingle investor claims across strategies with
  different risk/return profiles under one token — an actuarially messy
  claim, the kind of structure that invites exactly the "what am I actually
  buying" scrutiny that sank confidence in Falcon (§9d).
- **The strategy layer is exactly right.** A strategy already has its own
  isolated config, session journal, dry-run history, and even its own
  shutdown-policy override (architecture doc §5) — i.e. it already has a
  clean, separable track record before tokenization is even considered.
  This maps directly onto Enzyme's one-Vault-per-strategy model and GLAM's
  one-Vault+Mint-per-strategy model: the strategy's own Swig-secured wallet
  becomes its capital container, and a Mint (or a ported `fund-protocol`
  equivalent) becomes its investor-claim layer, one per strategy.

### 11c. The "more opinionated Condor, others build their own" instinct is right — just one layer down from where it was framed

Condor-the-assistant should stay the single, opinionated reference
coordinator/UX — exactly its current design ("you are the single
interactive agent," architecture doc §1) — rather than something users fork
into "their own assistant." But the instinct behind the question is
correct one level down: the white-label opportunity is at the
**agent/strategy layer**, not the assistant layer. This mirrors Enzyme
exactly — Enzyme itself doesn't ask anyone to use "Enzyme" as a brand;
Avantgarde, Yearn, Stader, and CV5 Capital each build their own branded
product on Enzyme's shared engine underneath. The equivalent for Condor:
Botcamp Solutions clients (or eventually external builders) create their own
branded **agents and strategies** on Condor's shared engine — the session/
runtime, Swig integration, and (per §11a) the shared skills/routines
library — while Condor itself remains the default reference experience, not
the thing being white-labeled.

### 11d. What doesn't need to change: Shape A requires none of this

Worth being explicit about scope: Shape A (§9b) — Swig-secured custody
within the existing hosted product — touches only the capital-custody
mechanism for a given strategy's execution wallet. It requires no ontology
change at all: no global skills tier, no strategy-as-tokenization-unit
design, no Mint/share-token layer. That's part of why it's the right
Stage 1 (§7) — it's architecturally cheap as well as legally low-risk. §11a–c
only become load-bearing if/when Stage 2 moves toward a genuine white-label
or tokenized-platform direction (§9's Shape B, or the Enzyme-partnership
option (c) in §7) — they are not prerequisites for validating Shape A.

## 12. Devin/Cognition's cloud-agent lessons: open-source is a non-issue; hosting architecture is the real question

Direct answer to the follow-up: read Cognition's
["Devin for Terminal"](https://cognition.com/blog/devin-for-terminal) and
["What we learned building cloud agents"](https://cognition.com/blog/what-we-learned-building-cloud-agents)
and decide whether Condor should stay open source or copy Devin's approach.

**These two posts don't actually bear on the open/closed-source question at
all.** Devin has no open-source component, and neither post makes a
licensing or business-model claim — the CLI post is silent on pricing and
distribution beyond an install command. Devin is a fully proprietary,
VC-funded agent product built by a company whose entire business *is* cloud-
agent infrastructure; that's a different company shape than Condor's chosen
one (an open framework stewarded by a nonprofit, with a for-profit hosted
layer on top, per §3a/§4), not a counter-example to it. **§3a's recommendation
is unchanged: stay open source.** If anything, §10's moat analysis already
covers the "should we close the source to compete with well-funded closed
players" question directly, and concludes the moat was never the code.

**What the posts actually answer is the harder half of §3c/§5: not** ***whether***
**to host, but** ***how*** **— and that answer argues for caution, not for
matching Devin's original build.**

- **"What we learned building cloud agents" describes an enormous,
  multi-team infrastructure investment, not a side feature.** Cognition
  rejected containerized sandboxes specifically because they share a kernel
  — "no shared attack surface" required custom **microVM isolation**, which
  took **over a year of dedicated hypervisor engineering**. Hypervisor-level
  **snapshotting** (full memory/process/filesystem state, so an agent can go
  idle across a CI wait or a code-review gap and resume exactly where it left
  off) was, in their own words, harder to build than anything else in the
  stack. On top of that, orchestrating "hundreds of concurrent sessions"
  required three separate dedicated teams — orchestration, governance
  (permission inheritance + tamper-evident audit trails), and integrations —
  with **"over three quarters of dedicated engineering"** spent on
  orchestration alone. This is what it costs to do multi-tenant cloud-agent
  hosting *properly and safely* at real scale. It is not a scope Botcamp Inc.
  — an education-and-services business, not an infrastructure company — can
  or should try to replicate to ship §5's hosted tiers.
- **The stakes are arguably higher for Condor than for Devin, which makes
  cutting corners on isolation worse, not better.** Devin's blast radius if
  a sandbox is compromised is source code, CI credentials, and internal
  systems. Condor's hosted agents hold live exchange API keys and real
  trading capital — Cognition's own reason for rejecting shared-kernel
  containers ("an attacker... cannot take all our funds," almost verbatim
  Swig's own README language, see §9a) applies at least as strongly here.
  A homegrown, under-resourced attempt at shared multi-tenant isolation would
  be a worse trade for Condor than for Devin, not a better one.
- **Tellingly, Cognition's own newest flagship product doesn't lead with the
  cloud-fleet model it spent a year building — it leads with local-first.**
  Devin CLI ("Devin for Terminal") runs directly on the user's own machine
  against the user's own environment, with cloud used only as an *overflow*
  — "start locally, then hand the same session off to the cloud" once a
  workload outgrows the laptop, so the user can close it and keep going.
  Even the team with a working, hardened multi-tenant cloud-agent stack
  ships its headline product as local-execution-by-default with cloud as a
  premium escape hatch, not the reverse.

**Recommendation: don't copy original-Devin (build a shared, hypervisor-
isolated, multi-tenant cloud fleet); copy new-Devin (local-first by default,
cloud/hosted as a single-tenant convenience layer, not a shared elastic
fleet).** Concretely, this refines §5's hosted-instance architecture rather
than changing whether Condor hosts at all:

- **Every hosted tier — not just the "Enterprise / custom" tier — should be
  a dedicated, single-tenant VM or container per paying customer**, not
  shared multi-tenant infrastructure. This is a much smaller engineering
  problem than Cognition's: with no cross-tenant sharing, there's no
  kernel-escape attack surface to defend against in the first place, so
  ordinary off-the-shelf VPS/container hosting is sufficient — no custom
  hypervisor engineering required. This is also not a new idea to invent:
  it's already how Condor's own session architecture works today
  (architecture doc §6 — one dedicated OS process tree per chat session),
  and Phase 2's roadmap already proposed exactly this pattern, just scoped
  too narrowly to the enterprise tier alone (see roadmap-v2.md's Phase 2,
  which now applies this pattern to every hosted tier, not just Enterprise).
- **Skip building Cognition's hardest problem — hypervisor-level
  snapshot/resume across unpredictable idle gaps — because Condor doesn't
  actually have that problem.** Devin needed it because a coding agent's
  workflow has genuine unpredictable async gaps (waiting on CI, on a human
  reviewer). A Condor `TickEngine` strategy is periodic and effectively
  always-on, not idle-then-resume; the existing shutdown-policy sequence
  (architecture doc §5) and the health-monitor's dead-session cleanup
  (`handlers/agents/session.py`'s 15-second sweep) already cover Condor's
  simpler restart needs. Building Devin-grade snapshotting would be solving
  a problem Condor's workload doesn't have.
- **Skip building Devin-grade orchestration/governance/integration layers
  until scale actually demands it.** Cognition built three dedicated teams
  to manage *thousands* of concurrent VMs. Phase 1/2's actual target
  (Botcamp Solutions' 3 named mandates, then a modest external prosumer
  base per §3b) is orders of magnitude below that threshold — a
  single-tenant-VM-per-customer model plus the existing health monitor is
  proportionate today. This is a "revisit if/when we approach Devin's
  scale" item, not a Phase 1/2 build requirement.
- **This actually strengthens the sequencing already recommended in §7.**
  Shape A (Swig-secured custody, dogfooded internally first) requires no
  multi-tenant isolation story at all — it's already single-tenant by
  construction, one customer's mandate per hosted instance. The Devin
  lesson is a reason to be *more* confident in shipping Stage 1 as scoped,
  and a reason to treat "build a shared elastic cloud fleet" as explicitly
  out of scope until real usage volume — not engineering ambition — demands
  it.

### 12a. Does this invalidate Phase 2's hosted-instance strategy?

No — but it's worth checking directly rather than assuming, since it's a
fair question: if even Cognition's own users pulled it back toward
local-first, why should Condor build a hosted tier at all?

**The reason doesn't transfer, because the underlying problem is different.**
Devin CLI's rationale for local-first is that a *coding* agent benefits from
running inside the user's own environment — their repo, their installed
toolchain, their git/registry credentials, their existing dev context — none
of which a cloud sandbox can cheaply replicate. That's a real, specific
reason, not a generic "cloud agents are bad" conclusion. It doesn't apply to
what §5's hosted tier actually sells: keeping a `TickEngine` strategy loop
alive at 2am. There's no "your own environment" advantage to that task —
if anything a user's own laptop is a *worse* place to run an always-on
trading strategy than a data-center box (worse uptime, worse network,
someone has to keep it powered on). Running a trading bot on a rented VPS
predates AI agents entirely; it's the default pattern Hummingbot users
already follow themselves. Applied consistently rather than superficially,
"put agent compute where its natural execution environment already lives"
argues *for* hosting the always-on tick-loop piece, not against it.

**Condor's Phase 2 design was already the hybrid model, not the pure-cloud
one.** §5 keeps self-hosted free forever and scopes the paid tier
specifically to the piece that benefits from being hosted — that's
structurally the same shape as Devin CLI's "local by default, cloud only for
the part that needs it," not the shared-fleet model Cognition built first.
Also worth noting: Cognition didn't shut down its cloud product to ship
CLI — Devin proper, with real enterprise results (Itaú: nearly 17,000
engineers, 5–6x faster migrations, doubled test coverage in eleven months),
keeps running alongside it. CLI is additive for a different task shape, not
a retraction of the cloud business — if anything the Itaú result is
evidence that a hosted-agent business has real ceiling when scoped and built
correctly, which is the actual cautionary point of the whole post.

**The one real caution, and it's about discipline, not direction.** That
Cognition — a well-funded team whose entire business is cloud-agent infra —
still needed a "lessons learned" post at all is a signal that multi-tenant
cloud agent infrastructure is an easy scope to drift into accidentally. The
risk isn't "Phase 2 shouldn't exist," it's "Phase 2 should stay disciplined
about staying single-tenant-VM-per-customer (§12 above) rather than drifting
toward 'platform' language — API tokens, a shared control plane, elastic
scaling — that quietly re-creates Cognition's hardest problems before
there's real usage volume to justify it." Reread Phase 2's roadmap scope
periodically against this test, not just at kickoff.

## 13. Accumulated intelligence: an opt-in shared learnings store as a new network-effect moat

`docs/architecture/agent-framework.md` documents `learnings.md` and
per-session journals as a **private, per-strategy** accumulation (§4 there;
also `mcp-first-value-add.md` §4 point 2, which already names this
accumulated track record as a defensible asset). This section considers a
distinct, additive idea: what if users could **opt in** to share a redacted
version of their strategy's performance and learnings with a central Condor
service, queryable by anyone (including brand-new strategies) building a new
agent? That converts a purely private, single-tenant asset into a
**cross-tenant network-effect moat** — a fifth item alongside §10's existing
list (brand/community, hosted-ops convenience, marketplace network effects,
captured capital/liquidity network effects), and arguably the sharpest one
available that doesn't require any capital custody or securities exposure.

**Why this is a genuinely different, stronger moat than the private
track-record asset already identified**: a private per-strategy journal
only benefits its own strategy. A shared, aggregated corpus benefits *every*
new strategy from day one, and — critically — it compounds precisely
because it can't be forked. A competitor cloning Condor's codebase gets
identical software and zero rows of accumulated learnings; the corpus only
exists because real users, running real strategies against real capital,
chose to contribute to it over time. This is the same "captured network
effect, not code" logic as §10's capital/liquidity point, applied to
*data* instead of capital — and it requires none of Shape B's securities
exposure, since no tokens or investor claims are involved.

**Design principles, if pursued:**

- **Opt-in, not default-on**, and ideally reciprocal ("give to get"): access
  to the full, non-aggregate corpus reserved for contributors, with a
  free/degraded tier (coarse aggregate stats only, no raw text) available
  to non-contributors — closer to a data co-op norm (cf. threat-intel
  sharing consortiums, Waze) than a one-way telemetry opt-in, and it
  directly counters the obvious adverse-selection risk: without a
  reciprocity mechanic, the strategies most likely to opt in are the ones
  doing worst (looking for help) while the highest-performing strategies
  have the least incentive to share their edge.
- **Redact before publish, not after.** Given Condor's own existing
  philosophy of human-confirmation-gated actions (`consult` vs. auto-
  approved `delegate`, architecture doc §1), sharing should follow the same
  pattern: a strategy's `learnings.md` entries get reviewed/redacted (strip
  exact position sizes, wallet addresses, exchange account identifiers,
  anything specific enough to leak edge) before publication, not
  scraped wholesale. Concretely, add a `share_learnings: true` flag to
  `strategy.md`'s frontmatter (architecture doc §4) that opts a strategy
  into a redaction-then-publish pipeline, distinct from the flag simply
  making the private data public.
- **Tiered content, not one corpus**: coarse aggregate statistics (e.g. "N
  strategies of type X on exchange Y report distribution of drawdown under
  regime Z") carry little leakage risk and can be broadly available;
  richer textual learnings ("widening spreads 2x during high-funding-rate
  periods reduced adverse selection") are more valuable and more sensitive,
  and should sit behind the contributor-reciprocity gate above.
- **Reputation-weight contributions.** Not all learnings are equally
  trustworthy — weight or flag entries by the contributing strategy's own
  track record (tenure, uptime, whether it's running real capital vs. a
  dry run/backtest per architecture doc §4's experiment modes), so the
  corpus doesn't get diluted by noisy or paper-trading-only submissions.
- **Serve it as a new MCP tool**, e.g. `query_shared_learnings(tags,
  agent_type, regime, ...)`, callable by any strategy's `TickEngine`
  reasoning or by a Condor/domain-agent chat session — architecturally a
  small, natural addition to the existing MCP tool surface
  (`mcp_servers/condor/tools/`), not a new integration paradigm.

**Concrete use cases this enables:**

- **Faster cold-start for new strategies.** A newly deployed PMM strategy on
  an unseen exchange/pair queries the shared corpus for what other PMM
  strategies have observed about regime-dependent spread behavior, instead
  of learning purely from its own from-scratch experience — directly
  valuable to the Phase 1 agent-template effort (roadmap-v2.md's Condor
  Refactor, "Pre-defined agent templates").
  Similarly a fresh USDM-related strategy could bootstrap from other
  USDM-mandate strategies' funding-rate-drift learnings rather than
  starting cold.
- **Cross-strategy pattern discovery.** Learnings tagged by market regime
  become a semantic-search corpus (embeddings over redacted `learnings.md`
  entries) — a genuine RAG-over-trading-experience capability no single
  private strategy journal can offer, with a "sensor network" effect:
  patterns invisible to any one strategy (e.g. a funding-rate-drift signal
  that only shows up once several independent USDM-adjacent strategies'
  fragments are combined) become visible in aggregate.
- **A direct upsell for the hosted product (§5).** Read access to the full
  contributor-tier corpus is a natural premium feature — "the more
  strategies run on Condor, the smarter every new one starts" is a
  flywheel pitch a fork can't replicate on day one, since it has zero
  accumulated corpus regardless of code parity.
- **A future asset beyond raw text**: a large enough, well-curated corpus
  could eventually seed a purpose-built regime classifier or a fine-tuned
  small model — a durable technical asset distinct from (and outlasting)
  whatever LLM or harness happens to be in use at the time, consistent with
  `mcp-first-value-add.md`'s framing that the model/harness layer is
  commodity but accumulated content is not.

**Real risks worth naming, not glossing over:**

- **Correlated/herding behavior.** If many hosted strategies read the same
  shared learning and mechanically act on it simultaneously (e.g. many bots
  widening spreads in response to the same "high volatility" signal at
  once), that's a new, emergent systemic risk category the private-journal
  model didn't have. Mitigate by keeping the strategy's own LLM reasoning
  in the loop to interpret and apply a shared learning contextually rather
  than treating it as a deterministic trigger, and consider staggering how
  quickly newly published learnings propagate into live decision loops.
- **A different regulatory question than tokenization's securities
  exposure**: centrally aggregating and — if monetized as a paid tier —
  *selling* access to cross-user trading performance/learnings data starts
  to resemble distributing paid trading research, which can implicate
  investment-adviser regulation (not securities law, a distinct regime from
  §9's tokenization concerns) depending on jurisdiction and how prescriptive
  the shared content gets. Needs its own legal review before a paid tier
  ships, separate from any Shape B review.
- **Leakage/competitive risk** is the reason the redaction-before-publish
  design (above) has to be taken seriously, not treated as a formality —
  Botcamp Solutions' actual named clients (Ripple, MetaDAO, USDM1) would
  reasonably object to any design that could leak their specific operating
  parameters, even unintentionally, through an aggregated corpus.

**Recommendation**: worth building directly into Phase 1 (see
roadmap-v2.md's "Shared Intelligence" section, which now ships this
alongside the Condor Refactor rather than bridging to Phase 2) — it's a
genuine, additive moat that requires no capital custody and no securities
exposure, unlike §9's Shape B, and it directly strengthens the
hosted-instances pitch in §5. But ship the
opt-in/redaction/reciprocity design *before* any data collection starts, not
as a retrofit — once specific users' data is out, the same "general
solicitation"-style mistake pattern from §9d (moving fast on a
capital/data-sensitive mechanism before the guardrails are actually in
place) becomes possible here too, just with data leakage instead of a
securities violation as the failure mode.
